Belgium: 1960-2000

1960s - Language Conflict

The linguistic divide between Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia intensified. The "Leuven crisis" (1968) over language use at the Catholic University led to the institution's split. The language border was officially fixed (1962-1963). Congo gained independence (1960), ending Belgium's colonial era amid violence. Economic growth continued, with Brussels becoming a major European administrative center. NATO headquarters moved to Brussels (1967) after France withdrew from military command.

1970s - Federalization Begins

Constitutional reforms (1970) began Belgium's transformation into a federal state, creating cultural communities. Economic crisis hit Wallonia's traditional industries hard while Flanders prospered. The Egmont Pact (1977) proposed further devolution but collapsed. King Baudouin remained a unifying figure. Terrorism struck with attacks by the Communist Combatant Cells. Brussels grew as the de facto capital of the European Economic Community.

1980s - Federal State Emerges

Major constitutional reforms (1980, 1988-1989) created regions (Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels) with significant autonomy. The Heysel Stadium disaster (1985) killed 39 people during a European Cup final. Economic restructuring continued, with Flanders outpacing Wallonia. The far-right Vlaams Blok gained support in Flanders. Child abuse scandals began to emerge. Belgium remained central to European institutions as the EC expanded.

1990s - Crises and Reform

The Marc Dutroux child abuse case (1996) sparked massive protests and a crisis of confidence in institutions. The "White March" drew 300,000 protesters. King Baudouin died (1993); King Albert II succeeded him. Further federalization (1993) made Belgium officially federal. The dioxin crisis (1999) contaminated food supplies. Economic integration with the EU deepened; Belgium adopted the euro (1999). Political fragmentation increased along linguistic lines. Brussels became the primary seat of EU institutions.
